NOTE-TAKING
=====================================================================

[N1] Effective Note-Taking in Class - UNC Learning Center
URL: https://learningcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/effective-note-taking-in-class/
Why it matters
- Taking notes in class aids focus, listening, comprehension, and retention.
- Clear, concise notes save time later; messy or overly wordy notes cost time.
Before class
- Preview assigned reading and check the syllabus to anticipate main ideas.
- Skim the previous session's notes for context.
- Label each page with course and date; keep each course's notes together,
  in chronological order.
During class
- For conceptual material, capture main points, not every word.
- For factual material, near-verbatim notes can help short-answer recall,
  but only if reviewed within ~24 hours.
- Write down questions and confusing points to follow up on.
- Jot keywords, names, dates to define later.
- Use bullets, phrases, abbreviations, symbols - not full sentences.
- Use one consistent format day to day.
- Online lectures: watch through first, then rewind to fill gaps; note
  timestamps of parts to revisit.
Spotting what's important
- Intros and conclusions often summarize main points.
- Signal phrases ("there are three main...", "in summary..."), repetition,
  and emphasis/gestures flag key content.
Formats: Cornell, outline, flowchart/concept map, charting, sentence method.
Handwritten vs. digital
- Handwritten: easier diagrams, fewer distractions, may help conceptual
  learning.
- Digital: faster, higher volume, easy to edit/search/share, may help with
  factual content.
After class
- Revisit notes within a day or two: clarify, define terms, fill gaps,
  reorganize, note open questions.
- Summarize in your own words, write questions, chunk into categories.
- Turn notes into a study guide and self-test.
- Use office hours and academic coaching when stuck.

[N2] Note-Taking Methods: 10 Styles for Students - Adobe Acrobat
URL: https://www.adobe.com/acrobat/resources/note-taking-methods.html
Benefits claimed: focus, retention, organization, critical thinking via
rephrasing. Useful during lectures, while reading, and in group discussions.
Methods
1. Cornell - notes / cues / summary sections; good for review & retention.
2. Outline - bullets + indentation for hierarchy; suits linear thinkers.
3. Charting - table of categories (columns) x items (rows); good for
   comparisons and data-heavy content (stats, timelines).
4. Boxing - each topic in its own box; visual, easy to scan.
5. Flow - non-linear; ideas placed anywhere and linked with arrows; good
   for brainstorming and seeing relationships.
6. Mapping - central topic with branches for subtopics/details.
7. Sentence - one key point per line, numbered if needed; fast for
   quick-paced lectures.
8. Progressive summarization - highlight on first pass, distill further on
   later passes, end with a condensed summary.
9. AI-assisted - tools that summarize or let you query documents.
10. Hybrid - a primary method plus elements of others.
Choosing a method
- By goal: retention -> Cornell/outline; understanding -> mapping/flow;
  organization -> digital or hybrid.
- (The article also matches methods to "visual/auditory/kinesthetic"
  learner types; note that this framing is popular but not strongly
  supported by research.)

[N3] Taking Notes While Reading - UNC Learning Center
URL: https://learningcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/taking-notes-while-reading/
Core idea
- Copying large amounts of text and over-highlighting are passive and
  don't engage the brain; they cost time without much learning.
Strategies
- Notes from memory: read a short chunk (a paragraph to a page), pause,
  write what you remember, then check the text to fill in details. Slower
  but improves comprehension.
- Mark the text: underline/circle key terms; annotate margins with symbols
  and own-words summaries (or on a separate sheet for digital texts).
- Cornell layout: right 2/3 notes, left 1/3 keywords/questions, bottom
  strip for a page summary.
- Apps: Evernote, OneNote (free for UNC students) for synced, organized
  notes.
- Graphic organizers / concept maps: tables, flowcharts, timelines, Venn
  diagrams - good for science/history and visual material.
- Turn headings into questions before reading, then answer them as you go.
Tips
- Write in your own words; avoid over-highlighting.
- Wait until the end of a page/section before writing.
- Keep notes brief and focused on main ideas.
- Preview the chapter's structure first.

[N4] The Best Note-Taking Methods for College Students - Goodnotes
URL: https://www.goodnotes.com/blog/note-taking-methods
Seven methods, each with a best use:
1. Outline - nested bullets, ~1 sentence each. Best for fast live capture
   (lectures). Weak for diagram-heavy subjects.
2. Cornell (Walter Pauk, Cornell, 1950s) - main column, cue/question
   column, summary at bottom. Writing the summary aids understanding;
   takes more setup effort.
3. Boxing - each subtopic in a labeled box. Best for revision summaries;
   too slow for live lectures.
4. Charting - rows = items, columns = shared properties. Great for
   comparisons; poor for narrative/linear content.
5. Mapping - topic branches into subtopics. Good when points need
   explanation and to show relationships; can run out of space.
6. Zettelkasten - one idea per card, linked by tags, built up over time
   into a personal knowledge base. Not for live lecture capture.
7. Mind mapping - central idea with free-flowing branches. Good for
   brainstorming and revision; not for recording lectures live.
Rule of thumb: outline/Cornell for live capture; boxing/charting for
revision; Zettelkasten/mind maps for building ideas over time.

[S2] Studying 101: Study Smarter Not Harder - UNC Learning Center
URL: https://learningcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/studying-101-study-smarter-not-harder/
Reading is not studying
- Rereading, highlighting, and rote memorization are weakly linked to
  learning; rereading leads to fast forgetting.
- Active studying: build a topic study guide with questions and full
  answers; make your own quiz; explain aloud as if teaching; make examples
  from your own life; draw concept maps; work problems and explain each
  step; analyze in terms of question -> evidence -> conclusion.
- Organize materials by topic (use syllabus subtopics) before reviewing.
Study Cycle (Frank Christ): preview -> attend class -> review -> study ->
  check understanding. Skipping steps (e.g., skipping reading because it's
  covered in class) loses repetition and spacing.
Spacing
- Distributed practice: short sessions on each class across days/weeks
  beats marathon sessions, for the same or less total time.
- Also reduces procrastination. Review flashcards for memorized material
  periodically through the day.
Intensity
- Short, focused 30-45 minute sessions with active methods (e.g.,
  self-testing), then a break.
Environment
- Silence isn't best for everyone; find your noise level. Active studying
  is often spoken aloud.
- Keep several study spots; switch when one stops working; notice when
  and where you focus best.
Problems
- In technical courses, working problems matters more than reading.
  Record worked examples from class, annotate steps, explain why they work.
Multitasking
- Hurts learning and increases time needed. Phone off, block sites,
  timed social-media breaks as rewards.
Teach it / self-quiz
- Explain in your own words with examples; quiz yourself; focus on the
  questions you miss; answer aloud.
Calendar control
- Weekly planning session: list goals per class, compare with available
  hours, triage readings (fully read the main source; skim supplements),
  schedule tasks, plan tomorrow before bed.
Downtime
- Use light weeks to get ahead; touch every class every week (ideally
  daily, ~30 min each).

[S3] 4 Tips for Establishing Good Study Habits - Vault (Kaitlin McManus, 2019)
URL: https://vault.com/blogs/admit-one-vaults-mba-law-school-and-college-blog/4-tips-for-establishing-good-study-habits
(Personal, anecdotal blog post; author learned these after high school
habits stopped working in college.)
1. Set aside time - find when you focus best, block it as study time, and
   hold yourself to it.
2. Get organized - keep to the syllabus yourself; one carry-around folder
   for current materials plus one folder per class at home; keep a
   calendar and visible due dates.
3. Do the reading - and annotate/understand it; it underpins discussion,
   papers, and exams, and is easy to skip because it's rarely checked.
4. Study group only if helpful - keep it to ~3-4 people; try a one-off
   session first; drop it if it becomes social time or members come
   unprepared.

USING AI FOR STUDYING
=====================================================================

[A1] Generative AI for Academic Study - UNC Learning Center
URL: https://learningcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/generative-ai-for-academic-study/
First: check each syllabus; instructors' AI policies differ. Authorized
use generally means AI helps you think, not thinks for you.
Good uses
- Explain concepts and how they connect; apply them to problems; compare
  and contrast; generate metaphors. Refine prompts (be specific, assign a
  persona).
- Generate practice exams, quizzes, study guides, flashcards, timelines.
  Self-testing is one of the best exam-prep methods.
- STEM: step-by-step solutions and practice problems. Checking AI output
  for errors is itself good metacognitive practice.
- Summaries: before reading (orientation) or after (check key points), but
  still read the full text; cross-check with other sources.
- Planning: build daily/weekly study schedules.
Pitfalls
- Can short-circuit active learning; check mastery without AI.
- Hallucinations, including fabricated citations/quotes - verify sources.
- Bias inherited from training data.
- Cost and privacy: UNC offers PromptLab (free, data-protected access to
  multiple models) and Microsoft Copilot via UNC's Microsoft license.
  Check privacy terms of other tools. Energy use concerns.
- Weak at precise computation; check calculations. Many near-identical
  generated practice problems can create a "fluency illusion."

[A2] AI and Your Learning: A Guide for Students - Stanford CTL
URL: https://ctl.stanford.edu/aimes/ai-learning-guide-students
Policy
- Review each course's AI policy. Stanford's guidance treats AI help like
  help from another person; using AI to substantially complete work is
  prohibited. Default: assume not allowed unless stated; disclose use.
  "When in doubt, ask and document."
How AI works / limits
- Generates statistically plausible text from patterns, not knowledge ->
  can hallucinate, carry training-data bias, misattribute or invent
  citations. Not a substitute for human support (tutors, coaches,
  advisors, counselors).
Learning principle
- Learning comes from what the student actively does and thinks.
- Deep strategies (compare/contrast, explain in own words, self-quiz)
  beat shallow ones (highlighting, rereading) - Dunlosky et al. 2013.
- Key question: does a given AI use deepen engagement or remove a chance
  to engage (e.g., AI previewing a reading for you)?
Questions to ask yourself
- Am I allowed? How must I document/disclose?
- How will I check accuracy and bias, and attribute sources?
- Does this use take away or add to deep engagement? Other factors
  (environment, privacy)?

GROUP STUDY
=====================================================================

[G1] Group Study - UNC Learning Center
URL: https://learningcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/group-study/
Why: accountability, more active strategies, support, community.
Who: 2-5 committed, prepared members; a facilitator or rotating "leader of
  the week" handles scheduling and keeps focus.
When: weekly, all semester (not just before exams); 60-90 min sessions.
Where: discussion-friendly but not loud; whiteboard and outlets; not overly
  comfy seating.
Setup: recruit 2-3 people; fix a recurring time (When2Meet/Doodle); book a
  room; ground rules (all questions welcome, take turns, no phones, come
  prepared, no judging, no competition); everyone knows prep tasks.
Session
- Prepare: agree on content and who presents which problems; favor
  conceptual questions over only application problems.
- Structure with SMART goals: review the week, compare notes, take turns
  teaching, bring questions, build concept maps/outlines/study guides,
  brainstorm likely exam questions.
- Mix problem types rather than blocking; vary modes (see/hear/do); write
  out solution steps.
- End with recall-based self-testing and a reflection on what to change.

[G2] Developing Effective Study Groups in the Quest for the "Holy Grail":
     Critical Thinking - Linn, Lord, Whong & Phillips, Am J Pharm Educ, 2013
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3806964/
(Letter to the editor / opinion piece in pharmacy education, not an
empirical study; authors note little published data on the topic.)
- Problem-solving study groups build critical thinking, listening, and
  persuasion skills that are hard to develop alone.
- Size ~4-6; members committed and responsible to each other; avoid a
  dominating member; maybe avoid close friends; agreed ground rules with
  consequences.
- Set explicit learning outcomes per meeting; assign each member a
  sub-problem/case to prepare and lead.
- Learn core knowledge individually first; nobody comes just to listen.
- Leader facilitates rather than lectures, doesn't give answers too fast,
  keeps time; take unresolved roadblocks back to the instructor.
- Introduce new information after a conclusion to force reassessment;
  role-play.
- Basic core knowledge may be better learned individually; groups shine
  for practicing critical thinking/problem solving.

STUDY APPS

[P1] The 25 Best Study Apps for College Students (2026) - Shovel
URL: https://shovelapp.io/the-25-best-study-apps-for-college-students/
(Published by Shovel, which ranks its own app first - treat as biased.)
First check: do you have enough study time at all? (Their calculator uses
  ~2+ hrs/credit/week as a baseline.)
Planners: Shovel (schedules work blocks, warns of time shortfalls), Notion
  (flexible workspace, templates), Todoist (to-do list, natural-language
  input), MyStudyLife (tracks classes/deadlines but doesn't plan time).
Notes: GoodNotes (iPad handwriting, PDF annotation, searchable
  handwriting), Obsidian (Markdown, linked notes, local files), OneNote
  (free, infinite canvas, synced audio), Evernote (web clipping, scanning),
  Notability (audio synced to handwriting, AI quizzes/flashcards).
AI: Perplexity (cited web answers), ChatGPT (explanations, practice
  questions), Grammarly (writing/tone), NotebookLM / "Gemini Notebook"
  (grounded summaries of your sources, audio overviews), Gamma (AI slides).
Memory & focus: Anki (spaced-repetition flashcards), Quizlet (flashcards,
  premade sets, quizzes), Forest (focus timer - virtual tree dies if you
  leave), Focusmate (virtual co-working via webcam), Cold Turkey (hard
  website/app blocker).
Life/other: Splitwise (shared expenses), YNAB (budgeting), Google
  Drive/Docs (collaboration), Mathway (step-by-step math), Tide (Pomodoro
  + ambient sound).
Advice: avoid tool overload - pick one app per category (planner, notes,
  memorization, research); consistency matters more than tools.

FLASHCARDS
=====================================================================

[F1] Flashcards - University of Toronto Scarborough Learning Strategies
URL: https://www.utsc.utoronto.ca/learningstrategies/flashcards
Why: combines retrieval practice with options for visuals, concrete
  examples, spacing, and chunking.
Making cards
- Less information per card.
- Use both directions (term -> definition and definition -> term).
- Add images or mnemonics.
- Shuffle within a course to mix topics.
- Formats: handwritten cue cards (optional hint in a corner you can cover),
  PowerPoint (odd slides = fronts, even = backs), Excel (hide answer
  column, color-code by confidence), Quizlet (premade sets may not match
  your course).
Using cards
- Work with 5-10 cards at a time; spend more time on weak cards; separate
  known ones.
- Start at least 2 weeks before a test.
- Cumulative method: learn card 1 until comfortable, add card 2 and
  recycle card 1, add card 3 and recycle 1-2, and so on - earlier cards
  get the most repetitions.
- Say front and back aloud; guess before checking.

[F2] The Ultimate Guide to Flash Card Making - Scholarly (2023)
URL: https://scholarly.so/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-flash-card-making
(Published by a company selling an AI flashcard tool.)
Best practices: keep cards concise; add visuals; group by topic; review on
  a regular schedule; shuffle to avoid relying on order.
Digital pros: quick to make, accessible anywhere, spaced-repetition
  algorithms, multimedia, progress tracking.
Digital cons: device distractions, limited customization in some apps,
  device dependence, setup time, switching between platforms.
Tools mentioned: Anki, Quizlet, Brainscape, Cram, Knowt, StudyStack,
  SuperMemo (also repurposing Notion/Evernote/Trello).
AI-generated cards: save time, but extraction accuracy is a concern - cards
  may include irrelevant or misleading content; privacy matters.
